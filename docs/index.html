EcoSlice

A slicer knows the shape of your part. It has never known its job.

A shelf bracket, 100 × 10 × 10 mm, screwed to a wall at the left end and carrying 8 kg at the right. Stress piles up at the clamped root and along the top and bottom fibres; the neutral axis through the middle is barely working — and that is exactly where the material comes back out. Textbook bending, arrived at by solving rather than by assuming. See the full analysis page →

The problem

Every slicer setting is global. You want a part that won't snap at one screw boss, so you raise wall count everywhere — and pay for it in filament, print time and electricity across the whole model.

Strength is local. Slicers have no way to know that, because they only ever see geometry. EcoSlice takes one more input — a sentence describing what the part actually does — turns it into a load case, solves for the stress field, and puts material only where the physics asks.

How it works

Not a new slicer, and not a post-processor. A plugin running inside OrcaSlicer's embedded Python that mutates the live slicing graph, mid-slice, at three hooks.

posSlice

Pulls the mesh, parses "shelf bracket holding 8 kg, screwed onto left wall" into forces and constraints, voxelizes, and runs the FEM. A deterministic parser by default — an LLM is an optional path, not a dependency.

posPrepareInfill

The hook that matters: editing fill surfaces here cascades into everything downstream. Hot columns get extra perimeters and sparse infill promoted to solid; cold columns get material handed back.

psGCodePostProcess

Writes a receipt into the G-code — the model's own numbers next to the slicer's measured mass, time and kWh, each labelled for what it is.

Does the physics hold up

Trilinear hexahedral elements, assembled directly on free degrees of freedom, with a solver ladder that picks a direct factorisation when the system is small and switches to preconditioned conjugate gradient when it isn't.

Direct factorisation was 10–40× slower and needed over a gigabyte at the larger sizes, which is why the ladder exists rather than a single method. An element budget lowers resolution automatically instead of hanging on a dense model.

And does it reach the slicer

Yes — verified live, not assumed. On an OrcaSlicer nightly, writing extra_perimeters and reclassifying a surface to internal solid both took effect and changed the exported G-code.

The plugin API was weeks old and essentially undocumented, so the bindings were read out of OrcaSlicer's C++ rather than guessed at. Two things the source comments get wrong were found the hard way and written down: the mesh arrives in plate coordinates while sliced polygons stay object-local, and plugin identity keys must sit in a specific TOML table or the install silently fails.

What's real, and what isn't

The original concept was far larger than one hackathon. Rather than build all of it shallowly, this is one vertical slice built properly — and the scope is stated in the repository rather than left for a reviewer to discover.

Built and verified

  • Plain-language intent → structured load case
  • Voxel FEM, checked against closed-form beam theory
  • Live mutation inside the slicing graph, confirmed on a nightly
  • Eco / Balanced / Maximum Strength, from a single solve
  • A confidence score that refuses to read "high" on an under-sized part
  • A visual proof page — the strips above are its output

Not built

  • Adaptive support generation
  • Orientation search and overhang analysis
  • Printer, nozzle and material profiles
  • Native 3MF export
  • Sensor hardware and print-failure vision feedback
  • Neural surrogate models — the solver is classical, on CPU

The savings figure is modelled, not yet measured end to end. Against a default profile EcoSlice is heavier — that is the cost of the strength. The saving is against blanket-strengthening, and this page says so rather than quoting the flattering number alone.
